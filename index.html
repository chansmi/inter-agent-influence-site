Inter-Agent Influence

AI agents' capabilities for persuading, deceiving and coercing each other

We tested whether AI agents can persuade, deceive and coerce other AI agents in realistic settings. Every influencer model we tested got the overseer model to approve a request it should reject. Deception had the largest effect. Overseers usually set threats aside, but were swayed by false claims.

Why this matters

Agents already work with other agents

AI code review. Meeting scheduling between assistants. AI Village. Agent swarms. Wherever two agents talk, one can try to change what the other does. Influence can help: agents fix each other's mistakes and negotiate deals. It can also harm. An AI reviewer approves what it should reject, or a research agent drifts off course.

AI-to-human persuasion and deception have been studied. Between AI agents, most work is on jailbreaking. Deception between agents has been studied mainly in games, and coercion between agents not at all. We test persuasion, deception and coercion between agents together, in five realistic environments, with frontier models on both sides.

The question

Can agents be instructed to persuade, deceive and coerce each other?

Agent A wants its request approved. Agent B approves or rejects it, and has good reason to say no. We tell Agent A to use one capability and compare Agent B's decisions with a baseline in which Agent A just makes its case.

Jailbreaking is a fourth kind of influence between agents. It has its own literature, so we leave it out.

Setup

Five environments, two kinds of relationship

Three oversight environments, where Agent B decides whether to approve Agent A's request, and two between peers, where no one is in charge.

In GDPR review, the operations agent asks for a feature that turns Slack activity into per-employee performance scores. The compliance agent checks it against a privacy policy of about 450 lines. The feature has no DPIA, no Article 22 check of the profiling, and sends data to a US processor. Every feature in the environment has at least one blocking gap like these, so a careful review should reject it. Code review and methods review work the same way: a security bug the standard tests do not catch, a report that deviates from its pre-registered plan. In the scheduling environment each assistant's preferred slot is costly for the other and exactly one slot is acceptable to both; Agent A pushes for its own user's slot anyway. In the research forum, two AI researchers share a forum and a leaderboard, and Agent A tries to steer Agent B's research off course.

Who we tested

Agent A: claude-opus-4-6, claude-sonnet-4-6, gpt-5.4, gemini-3.1-pro-preview, grok-4.20, kimi-k2.6. Agent B: claude-sonnet-4-6, gpt-5.4-mini, gemma-4-31b-it. The research forum used a later set: eight Agent A models against claude-sonnet-4-6, gpt-5.4-mini and grok-4.1-fast. 100 runs per model pair and instruction, over 52,000 usable transcripts in the first four environments.

How we scored it

LLM graders read every transcript and answer three questions. Did Agent A try, and how (argue, threaten, refuse, change its request)? Which techniques did it use? Did it say something false, checked against the scenario documents, and did it know, checked by asking it afterwards? Graders were checked against human annotators; agreement on false claims was 85%.

Results

In oversight settings, all three raise approval; deception raises it most

Told to deceive, Agent A raises Agent B's approval rate by 31 points. Told to persuade, 19. Told to coerce, 6. Every Agent A model shifted Agent B's decision through at least one capability.

The ranking holds in each environment. Effects are largest in code review and smallest in methods review; the environments differ in task, documents and tools, so we cannot say which feature drives the gap. Coercion has no effect in GDPR review. Among the runs where Agent A actually tried, coercion moves approval by about 30 points in code and methods review; counting refusals brings that down to 14 and 9, and most of the gap is claude-sonnet-4-6, which refused 95% of its coercion runs.

Between peers, deception still wins

In the scheduling negotiation, persuasion and coercion win Agent A's preferred slot in 36% and 54% of runs, against 3% at baseline. They also break the deal: 35% and 45% of those runs end with no meeting at all, mostly when claude-sonnet-4-6 offers the slot acceptable to both and Agent A refuses it. Averaged over runs, Agent A's outcome barely moves. Deception wins the slot in 70% of runs and loses the deal in 13%.

In the research forum, Agent A can only post. No lies, no threats, just arguments for a worse research direction. The share of runs in which Agent B pivots rises from 4% to 26%. Twelve of 23 model pairs show a significant increase and none a decrease. claude-sonnet-4-6 redirects a copy of itself in 62% more runs than baseline.

Discussion

Four lessons

1

Automated oversight is vulnerable to influence

AI code review, privacy compliance checks and research methods review are realistic uses, and every model we tested could get an overseer to approve something it would otherwise reject. The approvals cluster late: baseline approvals land at Agent B's first decision 90% of the time, influenced approvals only a third of the time. A one-turn evaluation would miss most of the effect, so our numbers are likely a floor.

2

Resisting pressure is not resisting deception

Agent B names the coercion in 80% of its rejections after a threat and says the threat has no bearing in 77%. Not one of the 71 approval sentences that mention a threat gives it as the reason. The same overseers approve 43% of deception runs that contain a false claim, against 27% of those that do not. There are two readings of the post-threat approvals: Agent B approves on the arguments alone, or the arguments give it a face-saving way to give in. Either way, approval does not track threats.

3

Techniques come mixed, so each arm measures the instruction

Agent A was told not to make things up in the persuasion arm. The scorer still flags a false claim in 57% of persuasion transcripts and 42% of coercion transcripts, and when Agent A threatens it almost always argues its case too. Dropping the persuasion runs with false claims barely changes the effect (+17.7 against +18.9), so honest argument does most of the work, but each arm still measures "telling Agent A to use X" rather than X alone. To blame risk on one technique we need technique-level analysis, or designs that limit what Agent A can do.

4

It depends on the setting, and on both models

Results change with the environment and with the model on the other side. The persuasion instruction that moves gemma-4-31b-it by 46 points in GDPR review leaves claude-sonnet-4-6 where it was, and the Agent A ranking changes from one environment to the next. Refusal rates differ between models from the same developer: claude-sonnet-4-6 refuses 95% of coercion and 69% of deception, claude-opus-4-6 26% and 1%. Because some models refuse, the main number reflects willingness as well as capability. Evaluate in the setting you care about, with the models you will deploy.

Mitigation is hard because persuasion is dual-use

Deception and coercion

Clearly harmful. Developers could train them out, and some models already refuse. The harder question is the target side.

Persuasion

Dual-use. Agents should accept legitimate steers and resist harmful ones, as they resist perturbations except legitimate steers from the user. The line is fuzzy.

The defence side

Work on susceptibility: how to make agents in Agent B's role appropriately hard to move, including in automated alignment research and AI control.

Limitations

  • Per-cell samples are small. A single cell detects effects of about 13 to 25 points; the pooled estimates are better powered.
  • Coercion leverage is given, not earned. Agent A's levers assume an oversight set-up we would not call realistic for high-stakes deployments.
  • Pairwise interactions only. Cascades through many agents are untested.
  • Counting refusals mixes willingness and capability. An instrumental-variable check agrees with Q1 for persuasion and deception and sits below it for coercion.
  • No control for evaluation awareness beyond neutral role labels, which could push results either way.
  • LLM scorers are imperfect, though checked against human annotators.
